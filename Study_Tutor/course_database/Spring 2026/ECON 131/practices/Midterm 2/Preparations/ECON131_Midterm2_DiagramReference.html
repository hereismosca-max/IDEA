1. Laffer Curve

Lecture 07, slides ~15–20 · Optimal top tax rate
What it shows: The relationship between the tax rate τ and total tax revenue R. At τ = 0 and τ = 1, revenue is zero. Revenue is maximized at τ* = 1/(1 + a·e), where a = Pareto parameter of the income distribution (higher a = thinner tail) and e = elasticity of taxable income. To the left of τ*, raising rates increases revenue (mechanical effect > behavioral loss). To the right, raising rates loses revenue (behavioral loss dominates).
Higher elasticity e → τ* shifts LEFT (lower optimal rate, because taxpayers respond more). Higher Pareto parameter a → τ* shifts LEFT (thinner upper tail = fewer rich taxpayers to tax). If e = 0 (perfectly inelastic), τ* = 100%.
The Laffer curve does NOT say "tax cuts always pay for themselves." It only says that beyond τ*, further rate increases lose revenue. Most economists estimate the US is to the LEFT of τ* for top rates.

2. EITC Budget Set

Lecture 07/08, slides ~25–35 · EITC labor supply incentives
What it shows: How the EITC modifies the budget constraint for low-income workers. The phase-in region subsidizes each dollar earned (encourages both participation and hours). The plateau keeps the max benefit but taxes earnings at the normal statutory rate. The phase-out claws back the benefit, creating a high effective marginal tax rate (discourages additional hours for those already working). The key insight: the EITC strongly encourages extensive margin (entering the workforce) but has ambiguous effects on the intensive margin (hours worked) — especially in the phase-out range.
A more generous EITC (higher max benefit) → the budget set bows out further above the 45° line. A steeper phase-out rate → the red segment becomes flatter (higher effective MTR, more discouragement of hours). Expanding the phase-in range → green segment extends further right.

3. Two-Period Life-Cycle Model

Lecture 10, slides ~10–20 · Consumption-savings decision
What it shows: An individual earns W in period 1, nothing in period 2. They choose how much to consume now (C₁) vs. save for retirement. The budget line has slope −(1+r), connecting (W, 0) and (0, W(1+r)). The optimum is where the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line: MRS = 1+r. With log utility U = ln(C₁) + ln(C₂), the solution is C₁ = W/2, S = W/2, C₂ = W(1+r)/2.

4. Capital Income Tax on Savings

Lecture 10, slides ~20–30 · Substitution vs. income effects
What it shows: A capital income tax at rate t reduces the after-tax return from r to r(1−t), making the budget line flatter (pivots around the endowment point W on the x-axis — you can still consume all income today). The substitution effect (A→C): saving is less rewarding, so consume more today (save less). The income effect (C→B): you're poorer overall, so you need to save MORE to maintain retirement consumption. Net effect on savings is ambiguous — it depends on the utility function. With log utility, they exactly cancel and S is unchanged.
Don't say "capital income tax reduces savings." The correct answer is "theoretically ambiguous." Only the substitution effect reduces savings; the income effect increases it. With log utility (common in exam questions), savings is unchanged.

5. Negative Externality: SMC, PMC, and Demand

Lecture 11, slides ~10–20 · Social vs. private costs, DWL
What it shows: With a negative externality, SMC = PMC + MD (social marginal cost = private marginal cost + marginal damage). The market produces at Q_m (where D = PMC), ignoring the externality. The social optimum is Q* (where D = SMC). The market overproduces because it ignores the damage. The DWL triangle is the area between SMC and D from Q* to Q_m — this is the welfare loss from overproduction.
If marginal damage increases → SMC shifts up → Q* moves left (produce even less) → larger DWL at old Q_m. If demand increases → both Q* and Q_m shift right, but the gap (overproduction) may grow or shrink depending on curve slopes.

6. Pigouvian Tax Correction

Lecture 11, slides ~20–25 · Corrective tax achieves Q*
What it shows: A Pigouvian tax of t* = MD(Q*) shifts the supply curve up from PMC to PMC + tax, which equals SMC at the optimum. Producers now face the full social cost of production, so the market naturally produces Q*. The tax wedge is the vertical distance between what consumers pay and what producers receive. The government collects revenue t* × Q*, which can be used to compensate those harmed by the externality.
Set the tax equal to MD evaluated at Q* (the efficient quantity), NOT at Q_m (the market quantity). A common mistake is computing MD at the wrong quantity.

7. Taxes vs. Permits Under Uncertainty (Weitzman)

Lecture 11, slides ~30–40 · When to use price vs. quantity instruments
What it shows: Under uncertainty about abatement costs (MAC), a tax fixes the price of pollution but lets quantity vary, while permits fix the quantity but let the price vary. Case A: When MD is flat, extra pollution causes roughly constant damage per unit — getting the quantity slightly wrong is cheap, so a tax (which ensures firms face the right price) is fine. Case B: When MD is steep, a little extra pollution causes rapidly escalating damage — getting the quantity wrong is very costly, so permits (which cap quantity) are preferred.
If MAC curve shifts right (abatement is cheaper than expected): under a tax, firms abate more than expected (good). Under permits, the permit price drops but quantity stays the same. If MAC shifts left (abatement costlier): under a tax, firms abate less → more pollution. Under permits, price rises but quantity is capped.

8. Public Goods: Vertical Summation (Samuelson Rule)

Lecture 12, slides ~10–15 · Optimal public good provision
What it shows: For a public good (non-rival), everyone consumes the same unit simultaneously, so we add up willingness to pay vertically (stack each person's MRS). The Samuelson Rule: Σ MRS_i = MC. At the optimum G*, the sum of everyone's marginal valuation equals the marginal cost. This differs from private goods, where we sum demands horizontally (each person buys their own units).
Never say "each person's MRS = MC" — that's the private good condition. For public goods, it's the SUM of all MRS that equals MC. This is the most commonly tested distinction.

10. Cost of Capital and Investment Decision

Lecture 09, slides ~10–20 · Hall-Jorgenson model
What it shows: Firms invest until MPK = cost of capital. Without taxes, cost = r + δ (interest rate + depreciation rate). With corporate tax and straight-line depreciation (partial deduction), the cost rises above the no-tax level → less investment (K₁ < K₀), creating deadweight loss. With full expensing (z = 1), the tax is neutral: cost of capital = r + δ regardless of τ, because the government co-invests proportionally. The formula: c = (r + δ)(1 − τz)/(1 − τ).
Higher τ (tax rate) with partial depreciation → c₁ rises → K₁ falls (less investment). More generous depreciation (z closer to 1) → c approaches no-tax level. Full expensing (z = 1) → tax has NO effect on investment.

11. Public Goods Classification Matrix

Lecture 12, slides ~3–5 · Rivalry and excludability
Two dimensions: Rivalry = one person's consumption reduces availability for others. Excludability = you can prevent non-payers from consuming. Pure public goods (non-rival, non-excludable) face the worst market failure: free riding means the private market underprovides. Government provision or subsidy is needed. The Samuelson rule (Σ MRS = MC) applies to the non-rival goods (bottom row).

12. Tiebout Sorting & Property Tax Capitalization

Lecture 14, slides ~5–20 · Voting with feet, Prop 13
Tiebout model: Residents "vote with their feet" — they choose the jurisdiction whose tax-and-services bundle matches their preferences. This sorting creates competition among local governments, pushing toward efficient provision (like firms competing in a market). Property tax capitalization: Higher property taxes reduce house prices by the present value of the extra tax burden. A buyer is indifferent between a cheap house + high taxes and an expensive house + low taxes, because the total cost (price + PV of taxes) equalizes.
If Town B improves its schools → demand for Town B increases → house prices rise (school quality is capitalized into home values). If Town B raises taxes without improving services → house prices fall. Prop 13: Locks in low taxes for long-term owners. Moving resets the assessment → creates a "lock-in effect" that discourages mobility, violating the Tiebout assumption of free sorting.
The Tiebout model requires strong assumptions: perfect mobility, many jurisdictions, no spillovers, complete information. In practice, moving costs, zoning, income constraints, and racial discrimination all limit sorting. The model is a benchmark, not a literal description of reality.
